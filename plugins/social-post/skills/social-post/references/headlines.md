# Headlines

Read this before writing any card's headline or the first line of a post. A
feed reader gives a card about a second and a half. In that time the headline
is the whole message, and the chart is the proof they glance at to believe it.

All examples use invented names and figures.

## The headline states the finding, with its number

A card headlined "Weekly sign-ups" makes the reader find the point. A card
headlined "Sign-ups doubled in six weeks, from 310 to 640" hands it to them,
and the chart underneath proves it. The finding travels with the picture into
a repost, a screenshot or a quote, because it is painted into it.

The headline and the units line split the work:

```
headline:  Sign-ups doubled in six weeks, from 310 to 640     ← the finding
units:     New accounts per week · Weeks 1–6, 2026            ← units, scope, window
```

**This is not licence to editorialise.** The test is whether the chart proves
the sentence:

| Write this | Not this | Why |
|:--|:--|:--|
| "Returns fell from 9.1% to 6.4% after the size guide went live" | "Our size guide is a game-changer" | The first can be read off the chart; the second is a verdict the reader should reach themselves |
| "Two regions make up 71% of the backlog" | "The backlog is out of control" | Same fact, but the second adds an opinion the data doesn't hold |
| "Sign-ups doubled in six weeks" | "Sign-ups doubled thanks to the new onboarding" | The cause is not in the data. Only write it if the user said it, and then in their words |

So adjectives, verdicts and causes stay out. Quantified findings go in. If you
cannot put a number or a specific comparison in the headline, there is no
finding to post yet. Say so, and ask what the user wants the post to show,
rather than posting a topic label.

## One finding per card

A headline that needs "and" to join two separate claims is two cards. "Sign-ups
doubled and churn fell" is two findings: give each its own card, or post the
one that leads and leave the other out.

An "and" that joins two halves of one claim is fine: "Sign-ups doubled while
spend held flat" is one finding about efficiency, and one chart can prove it.

## Check the arithmetic before you write the sentence

The sentence shape sounds right while the numbers say otherwise, and nobody
re-adds the column before posting. Work the figure out from the data first,
then write the sentence that fits it.

**Pick the comparison before the word.** For a rise over a run of periods
the start is the first period. The end is the latest period, unless the series
peaked earlier and fell back: then the end is the peak, named as such ("week 1
to the week 9 peak"), and the card shows the latest value too, on the units
line and the chart, so the fall isn't hidden. Every run gets the same two
ends from the same data. Never move an end to make a word come true:
starting at the lowest point to reach "doubled" is the comparison a reader
catches in the comments, and the post loses the figure it was built on. If the
word doesn't hold on these ends, change the word, not the ends, and don't
offer other ends as a way to keep the word.

- **"Doubled"** means the later value is 2.0× to about 2.2× the earlier one.
  310 → 640 is 2.06×, so "doubled" holds. "Tripled" works the same way. 310 → 590 is 1.9×: write
  "nearly doubled". 310 → 780 is 2.5×: give the multiple or the two values.
- **"Half of"**, **"a third of"**: compute the share. 46% is "nearly half",
  not "half".
- **A change in a rate is in points.** 9.1% → 6.4% is a fall of 2.7 points,
  or a 30% fall in the rate. Say which; never write "fell 2.7%".
- **"More than the next two combined"**, **"the biggest"**, **"every
  month"**: check it against every row, not just the ones you looked at.
- **Rounding:** round once, at the end, and say the same figure everywhere.
  If the card says 2.06×, the post says "doubled" or "2.1×", never "2.5×".

Every figure in the headline is one the reader could recompute from the data
the user gave. `tracing.md` is where that recomputation is shown on request.

## Length

The card's headline is set at 22px (17px on a landscape card) and has room for
two lines before it takes the chart's room:

| Card | Characters per line | Keep the headline under |
|:--|:--|:--|
| portrait, square | ~40 | ~95 |
| landscape | ~52 | ~80 |

Past that it runs to a third line, and each extra line costs the chart ~27px
(~21px on landscape). If a headline won't fit, it is usually carrying two
findings, or a qualifier that belongs in the units line.

## The first line of the post

The post's first line does the headline's job for the words. On LinkedIn and
Instagram the "…more" fold cuts the post at about 125–140 characters on a
phone, so for most readers the first line is the post. Lead with the same
finding and number as the card, in a sentence a person would say:

```
Sign-ups doubled in six weeks: 310 in week 1, 640 in week 6.
```

Not a teaser ("You won't believe what happened to our sign-ups"), not a stock
opener ("In today's fast-paced world…"), not a question to fish for comments.
`cards-and-posts.md` § The words lists what the checker warns on.

## When the figures are illustrative

A topic with no data gets figures labelled *illustrative* on the card's source
line **and** in the post. A reader who sees a chart on a feed assumes it is
measured, so the label goes where the picture goes. Numbers carried forward
from an older post are labelled with their date; nudging them to look current
is invention even though each one came from somewhere real.
